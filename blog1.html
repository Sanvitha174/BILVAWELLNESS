Why Quality Varies So Dramatically in Herbal Products

Unlike pharmaceutical drugs, where every tablet of a given medicine contains precisely the same active compound at a regulated dose, herbal supplements present a fundamentally more complex quality challenge. The potency of a herb depends on where it was grown, at what time of year it was harvested, how it was dried and stored, how it was processed into powder or extract, and whether the final product was tested before packaging.

A manufacturer who cuts costs at any one of these stages produces a product with unpredictable, often severely reduced potency. The consumer sees a capsule, reads a herb name on the label, pays a price and assumes the product works. Often, it does not, or not nearly as well as a properly manufactured equivalent. Worse, poorly stored or inadequately tested products can contain mould, heavy metals, pesticide residues or microbial contamination that pose genuine health risks.

The Core Problem

Because herbs are natural products, there is a widespread assumption that they are inherently safe and that quality is self-evident. Neither is true. The quality and safety of a herbal supplement depends entirely on the manufacturing process, the testing protocol and the honesty of the company making it. Certifications and standards exist specifically to make these invisible processes visible to consumers.

The good news is that India has a well-developed regulatory framework for herbal products. The challenge is that compliance is not always enforced uniformly, and marketing language can make a non-compliant product look indistinguishable from a fully certified one to an uninformed buyer. The sections below cut through this confusion clearly.

What GMP Certification Actually Means

🏭

Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)

The manufacturing quality standard every supplement facility must meet

GMP stands for Good Manufacturing Practice. It is a globally recognised system of quality standards and controls that govern every stage of how a supplement is produced, from raw material sourcing through manufacturing, testing, packaging and storage. In India, GMP standards for herbal and Ayurvedic products are defined under Schedule T of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, and enforced by State Licensing Authorities.

What a GMP audit actually checks: To achieve and maintain GMP certification, a manufacturing facility must demonstrate documented, verifiable compliance across a long list of requirements. These include: the physical cleanliness and design of the facility, the qualification of staff who handle raw materials and production, the calibration of all weighing and measuring equipment, the documented protocols for each stage of production, the procedures for identifying and segregating out-of-specification batches, and the records kept for every single production batch, which must be available for inspection.

Why it matters to you: A GMP-certified facility has been independently audited and approved. This means that what is stated on the product label accurately reflects what is inside the capsule or bottle, that the potency measured in testing is the potency you receive, and that the product was made in conditions that prevent contamination, mislabelling or adulteration. Without GMP certification, there is no independent verification of any of these things. A company can state any ingredient and any quantity on a label without having to prove it.

Schedule T compliant Batch testing mandatory Independent audit required Drugs and Cosmetics Act
How to verify: Ask the brand for their GMP licence number and the licensing authority that issued it. A legitimate brand will provide this without hesitation.

What AYUSH Approval Requires

✅

AYUSH Approval

India's regulatory framework for herbal and traditional health products

AYUSH stands for the Ministry of Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homeopathy, which is the Indian government body responsible for regulating traditional medicine and herbal health products. AYUSH approval means a product has been reviewed and licensed under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act by the relevant AYUSH licensing authority, confirming it meets India's safety, purity, labelling and manufacturing requirements for herbal health products.

What AYUSH licensing requires: To obtain an AYUSH manufacturing licence, a company must hold a valid GMP certification, employ qualified personnel including an Ayurvedic or pharmacy graduate for quality supervision, use ingredients that conform to the standards listed in the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India or the Pharmacopoeia of India, provide documentation of each ingredient's source and quality, and submit product formulations for review by the licensing authority before sale.

What AYUSH approval does not guarantee: AYUSH approval confirms a product meets the regulatory baseline for safety and labelling compliance. It does not independently verify specific health benefit claims made in marketing. This is why legitimate AYUSH-approved brands use language such as "supports," "helps maintain" and "may assist with," rather than "cures," "treats" or "eliminates." Any brand making definitive disease cure claims while claiming AYUSH approval is misrepresenting at least one of those facts.

Government licensing required Ingredient standard verification Formulation review before sale Ministry of AYUSH, India
How to verify: Look for the AYUSH licence number on the product label. The format is typically a state-specific licence code. You can contact the State Licensing Authority to verify any number you find.

Standardised Extracts vs Raw Herb Powders

This distinction is one of the most important and least understood factors in herbal supplement quality. It directly determines whether the product in your hand delivers a consistent, measurable dose of the active compounds your body needs, or an unpredictable quantity that varies batch to batch.

Factor Standardised Herbal Extract Raw Herb Powder
What it is Processed to guarantee a minimum percentage of the key active compound in every batch Simply dried and ground whole herb, with no potency guarantee
Potency consistency Consistent across batches regardless of growing season or origin variation Highly variable, depending on soil, rainfall, harvest timing and storage
Dose predictability You receive the same active dose every time Active compound content may vary by 30 to 80 percent between batches
Label verification Standardisation percentage is independently testable and verifiable No meaningful way to verify what is actually in the capsule without expensive testing
Bioavailability Higher, as active compounds are concentrated and often in a more absorbable form Lower on average, as active compounds are diluted within the whole herb matrix
Manufacturing cost Higher, due to extraction and standardisation processes Lower, which is why many budget brands use it
Research validity Clinical studies are conducted on standardised extracts, so results are applicable Raw powder products cannot validly claim benefits from standardised extract research
Practical Implication

When a clinical study demonstrates that Ashwagandha reduces cortisol by 27 percent, that study was conducted using a standardised extract containing a specific percentage of withanolides, typically 5 percent. A raw Ashwagandha powder capsule may contain a fraction of that withanolide content and has no legitimate basis to claim the same result. Always look for "standardised extract" and the standardisation percentage on the label.

How to Read a Herbal Product Label

A product label is a legal document in India under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act. It is required to contain specific information, and the presence or absence of certain fields tells you a great deal about a brand's commitment to transparency and compliance.

Batch number and manufacturing date A batch number links your specific product to a documented production record. This is mandatory under GMP. Expiry date Herbal extracts degrade over time. An expiry date confirms stability testing was conducted. Products without expiry dates have not been properly stability-tested and the brand is not complying with labelling law. Best Before: Apr 2028

Complete ingredient list with quantities Every ingredient, including both active herbs and excipients (binders, fillers, capsule material), must be listed. Quantities per serving should be stated. 6
Standardisation information For extracts, the label should state what the extract is standardised to and at what percentage. This is the proof that active compounds are present at a meaningful, consistent level. Ashwagandha Extract (standardised to 5% withanolides)
7
Directions for use and dosage Clear, specific dosage instructions confirm the brand has actually formulated the product with a target dose in mind, not simply filled capsules with whatever quantity was convenient. Take 1 to 2 capsules twice daily with warm water after meals
8
Disclaimer and cautions A responsible brand includes a disclaimer that the product is a supplement and not a substitute for medical treatment, and notes cautions for pregnancy, breastfeeding and medication interactions. Absence of this information is either carelessness or deliberate omission. Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Consult a physician before use if pregnant or on medication.

7 Red Flags That Expose a Low-Quality Brand

Even with the best intentions, consumers are regularly misled by herbal products that look credible but are not. Here are the seven most reliable warning signs:

🚩
Cure or guarantee language in marketing Phrases like "cures diabetes permanently," "eliminates asthma in 7 days," or "guaranteed results in 30 days" are not only scientifically unsound, they violate the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act and ASCI advertising guidelines. A brand making these claims is either uninformed about regulations or deliberately deceptive. Either way, do not buy from them.
🚩
No physical manufacturing address on the label A PO box, a distributor's office or simply a city name is not a manufacturing address. If you cannot locate the actual facility where the product is made, you have no basis for trusting anything about the manufacturing process.
🚩
No licence number or unverifiable licence number This is the single most important compliance check. Ask the brand directly for their AYUSH licence number and the authority that issued it. If they deflect, provide a number that does not match their state, or cannot explain it, move on immediately.
🚩
Prices dramatically below the market average GMP-certified manufacturing, standardised extract sourcing, batch testing, proper packaging and quality staff all cost money. A product priced at 30 to 50 percent below comparable certified products has almost certainly cut one or more of these corners. The savings you see on price are being paid for in quality you cannot see.
🚩
Ingredient quantities hidden in a "proprietary blend" Labelling a mixture of herbs as a "proprietary blend" and stating only the total weight of the blend, without individual ingredient quantities, is a practice used to hide underdosing. A brand confident in its formulation will state what is in each serving, clearly and individually.
🚩
No batch number or expiry date on the packaging These are not optional. They are legal requirements under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act for any licensed herbal product. A product missing either field is not in compliance and has not been manufactured with the traceability that GMP requires.
🚩
No response when you ask for quality documentation A legitimate, certified brand can and should provide their GMP licence number, their AYUSH licence number, the name of the standardised extracts used in their products and a general overview of their batch testing protocol. If a brand becomes evasive, defensive or simply does not respond to these questions, treat that response as your answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does GMP certified mean for herbal supplements?
GMP stands for Good Manufacturing Practice. It is a regulatory standard enforced under Schedule T of India's Drugs and Cosmetics Act. A GMP-certified facility has been independently audited and confirmed to meet requirements for cleanliness, process control, batch documentation and quality testing. It means the product matches what the label states.
What is AYUSH approval and why does it matter?
AYUSH is India's Ministry responsible for traditional medicine and herbal health products. AYUSH approval means a product has been reviewed and licensed under Indian law, confirming it meets baseline safety, purity and labelling requirements. It is a legally meaningful certification, not a marketing label. Look for a licence number, not just a logo.
What is the difference between a standardised herbal extract and raw herb powder?
Raw herb powder is simply dried and ground whole herb with no guaranteed potency. Standardised extract is processed to guarantee a specific minimum percentage of the key active compound in every batch, delivering consistent, measurable, verifiable potency. Clinical research on herbs is conducted using standardised extracts, not raw powders. Choose products that clearly state they use standardised extracts.
How do I verify whether a herbal brand is genuinely GMP certified?
Ask the brand directly for their GMP licence number and the State Licensing Authority that issued it. A genuine GMP-certified brand will provide this information immediately and without hesitation. You can also contact the relevant State Licensing Authority to verify the number independently.
Are all AYUSH-labelled products equally trustworthy?
No. AYUSH licensing establishes a regulatory baseline, but the quality of manufacturing, the potency of extracts used and the honesty of label claims vary significantly even among licensed brands. GMP certification, standardised extracts, full ingredient transparency and batch testing are the additional markers of a genuinely high-quality brand within the AYUSH-licensed space.
